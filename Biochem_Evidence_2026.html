1. The course was rebuilt twice, and most of the archive predates it

All six fall syllabi from 2021 to 2026 were read end to end. Two structural changes separate this year's course from the years most of the practice material came from.

Four exams became eight in Fall 2025. Through 2024 the course ran four examinations at 22.5% each, with 10% for quizzes and clickers together. The eight module exams that carry 90% of the grade today are one year old.

Genetics arrived at the same time. The 2021, 2022, 2023 and 2024 fall syllabi contain no mention at all of DNA replication, DNA repair, RNA types and synthesis, RNA processing, the genetic code or translation — zero occurrences in each of the four. The 2025 and 2026 syllabi carry thirteen and seventeen. That material was Medical Genetics, a separate spring course with its own syllabus and its own faculty. The 2025 syllabus says the course now “combines the disciplines of Medical Biochemistry and Medical Genetics”.

Fall 2025 is therefore structurally identical to Fall 2026 — the same eight modules, in the same order, with the same names and the same weights. Material dated 2025 is a direct match rather than an approximation, and is ranked accordingly throughout the weekly guide.

Everything older needs re-sorting by topic. Of the 13,711 extracted question items that carry a year, 6,924 — slightly over half — come from the four-exam years. Their own exam labels name a different exam from the one their content sits on now:

The four-exam boundaries also moved from year to year: cholesterol sat on Exam 3 in 2021 and on Exam 2 in 2024. A file named for an exam number is naming a boundary that has since moved twice, which is why every question in this survey was sorted by its content against the 2026 lecture list rather than by the label it arrived with.

What follows for how prior-year material is weightedThree things. MSK 1 and MSK 2 are the weakest-supported modules on the schedule, because almost all of their pre-2025 material was written for a different course with different faculty — so 2025 and 2026 material carries disproportionate weight there, and anything labelled “Genetics Exam” is a spring artefact rather than a head start. GI looks over-supplied because it inherited a 22.5% exam and now carries eight points; the volume of GI material is a fact about 2022, not about what is coming in November. And an exam number on a filename is not a routing key: the only reliable way to place an older file is by what it is about, which is how every list in the weekly guide is built.

Supply index is the module's share of the available questions divided by its share of the grade. Above 1.0 means more material exists than the marks justify. Rank correlation between points and question count is −0.881.

Why this sets the study orderGI carries 4.8% of the exam grade and 18.2% of the practice material; Foundations 2 carries 26.3% of the grade and 9.7% of the material. Why this shapes what to practiseTwo conclusions follow. First, since one option in five is a disease or enzyme name, the faculty's posted disease lists are effectively published answer sets, and memorising a fourteen-line list is a direct return. Second, student-written reviews are shorter, less numeric and almost never carry figures, so they are the right tool for covering breadth and the wrong tool for rehearsing the real format — that comes from the faculty reviews and the interactive decks. Answer-of-last-resort heuristics have nothing to work with here.

Why this drives the emphasis per examEach module rewards a different kind of preparation. Foundations 2 rewards an enzyme-regulator table and comfort with long figure-attached stems. MSK 2 and Renal reward working from presentation to a named condition or enzyme, which is why both get a table built for exactly that. CP 1 rewards drilling acid-base interpretation against a lab-value sheet. CP 2 has no dominant shape, so coverage of its two lists beats pattern practice.

Nine topics clear every filter — five or more years of recurrence, on the 2026 syllabus, and named in a posted faculty topic list: enzyme kinetics, electron transport and ROS, signal transduction, carbohydrate digestion, glycolysis, PDH and TCA, gluconeogenesis, glycogen, pentose phosphate. Eight of the nine belong to Foundations 1 and 2, the 72 points at the front of the semester.

Some topics pair across exam boundaries. Signal transduction from Foundations 1 co-occurs with acid-base on CP 1 27 times and with eicosanoids on CP 2 22 times. The genetic code on MSK 2 co-occurs with haemoglobin on CP 1 25 times, bridged by the sickle-cell point mutation. Translation and RNA synthesis co-occur 22 times across the two MSK exams.

Why signal transduction and haemoglobin get singled outSignal transduction is the highest-leverage single topic because it recurs in six years, is named on this year's topic list, and reappears on two later exams — August work that pays three times. Haemoglobin is the most consistently examined topic in the entire record, appearing in every year surveyed. Cross-boundary pairs are worth learning together rather than twice.

Among the student reviews, the Montana campus decks measure best: 71.7 questions per file at 46.6% complete, against Middletown at 57.6 and 27.1%, and Harlem at 45.0 and 23.1%. All three cover all eight modules.

Interactive-session decks need opening as slides. Four in five of their items lose their answer options when the text is extracted, because the options are drawn on the slide rather than typed. Their format is the closest match to the real exam, so the decks are worth opening in PowerPoint rather than reading as a list.

Why the source order is what it isFaculty reviews yield the highest proportion of workable questions of any source and demonstrably contain material that is not in the lecture slides, which puts them near the top despite being few in number. Student TA reviews are the only source that reaches nearly every topic and carry the least off-syllabus content, so they earn second place for breadth. Annotated study sheets have the highest off-syllabus rate of any type, which is why they are treated as marked-up slides rather than as a question bank.

Each assignment is corroborated by where the region's vocabulary appears in the question record: heme synthesis in 123 of 125 cases within GI, urea cycle in 112 of 119 within Renal, oxidative phosphorylation in 77 of 93 within Foundations 1.

Why the map is scheduled rather than learned up frontHalf the map belongs to one exam in December, and two exams worth 31 points between them use none of it. The single-carbon and sulfur amino acid region straddles GI and Renal because folate and B12 are taught in the water-soluble vitamin lecture, so learning it in November serves both. Steroid hormone synthesis has no lecture this semester and can be left alone. One annotated copy of the map circulating in the archive carries 260 annotations, and they sit almost entirely in the nucleotide and heme regions — making it a November and December resource rather than an August one.

Across all 33 decks only 5.5% of notes are off-syllabus, so the content is sound. The format is a different matter: card fronts run a median of 11 words against exam stems at 43, and 0.29% of cards present a clinical scenario against 20.0% of exam questions.

Deck coverage is distributed the same way the questions are. Notes per exam point: GI 88.4, MSK 1 86.6, Foundations 1 56.2, MSK 2 35.1, CP 1 33.4, Foundations 2 30.4, CP 2 19.8, Renal 14.5 — six times the density on the eight-point exam that the twenty-four-point exam has.

Why decks appear as a fact layer onlyCards work well for the things one option in five turns out to be: enzyme names, regulators, cofactors and disease associations. They cannot rehearse a fifty-word clinical stem with a figure attached, which is what Foundations 2 and MSK 2 actually ask. Seven decks are clean enough to keep; several others in circulation are 33% to 76% off-syllabus and belong to a different course.

10. What is not on the record yet

  • Six of the eight exams have no material dated this year. Only 19 items across four files carry a 2026 date, covering Foundations 1, Foundations 2 and GI. The interactive-session decks for the other modules are posted during the semester, which is why checking the current week's Canvas module is a standing task.
  • Everything forward-looking here is inferred from 2018–2025 material mapped onto this year's lecture list. The mapping is explicit and topic-by-topic; the transfer to a reorganised syllabus is an assumption.
  • Figures were recorded as present but never transcribed. The one-in-four figure load on Foundations 2 can be anticipated, but not rehearsed from question text — another reason to open the interactive decks as slides.
  • Some findings rest on small samples and are treated as indicative. The haemoglobin question shape comes from 19 complete items, and the DNA-block four-option pattern from 27. Both are reported as hints rather than plans.
  • The graded Canvas quizzes could not be surveyed. Their question banks are not readable, so nothing here describes them.
