How to work this page

Every module below follows the same shape: the weeks it lives in, then a numbered list of what to open in priority order with a link and a one-line reason, then what not to open, then the shape of question to practise, then the topics that carry the weight, then the last week before the exam. Read only the module you are on.

  • The order inside each list is the whole point. Position 1 is what to do if only one hour exists. Position 2 assumes 1 is done. Nothing below position 6 is worth reaching for before everything above it is finished.
  • Start every module from the faculty's own scope list. Those documents are two to four kilobytes each, they are written as testable objectives, and several bullets in them are already question stems. They are the shortest path from nothing to knowing what is examinable.
  • Time tracks exam points, not how much practice material happens to exist. The two are close to inverses of each other in this course, so working through what is available in the order it appears sends time to the wrong exams. The budget in the next card is the corrective.
  • Three passes per module, in this order: read the scope list and mark what you cannot yet do; work the lectures and the faculty review against that list; then work questions until the list is covered, and stop.

What to practise

Short items. Five options. No paragraph. On faculty-written questions about lectures 1–5 (n = 343 items, 81 complete): median stem 40 words, 39.9% at 50 words or more, 15.5% clinical vignettes — the lowest rate of any exam in the course, 71.4% containing a number, 74.1% five-option. On the 345 complete items routed here, mechanism-seeking language appears in 11.0%, regulation 4.1%, enzyme identity 2.9%, treatment 2.0%.
Two things the practice material will not make you do. The complete items routed here flag zero calculations and zero figure-reading questions, yet the topic list asks explicitly for an ETC energy-yield calculation. Do that calculation on paper from the topic list. And drill the double-reciprocal plot until the five inhibition patterns are automatic — all nine figures posted anywhere in this course are Foundations 1 material, four of them double-reciprocal plots, and the distinction worth over-practising is competitive against mixed, which separate only on whether the y-intercept moved.

What to practise

Long, figure-attached, regulation-focused. The most distinctive question shape in the course. On faculty-written questions about lectures 6–12 (n = 766 items, 99 complete): median stem 49 words, 75th percentile 75, and 49.6% of stems run 50 words or more — half the exam is a paragraph. 85.1% contain a number. 9.1% reference a figure and 24.4% carry an image; one question in four is attached to a graphic. 70.7% have five options.

On the 245 complete items routed here this is also the most regulation-heavy profile of the eight: regulation or rate-limiting step 9.4% (highest), enzyme or protein identity 8.6% (second highest), cofactor or vitamin 4.5% (highest), mechanism 10.6%, diagnosis 5.7%, figure reading 3.7%, calculation 0.8%.

So: a 50-to-75-word stem, often with a graph or a pathway fragment, giving enzyme activities or metabolite concentrations, asking which regulatory step is affected or which enzyme is deficient, with five options drawn from the posted disease list.
Two gaps to close deliberately. Calculation is flagged in 0.8% of the archived items here, but the faculty list names two ATP-yield calculations as objectives — the demand is real and the practice material under-represents it, so do the arithmetic from the list. And no figure anywhere in the archive was ever transcribed, so the one-in-four figure load can be anticipated but not rehearsed from question text. That is the strongest argument for opening the interactive decks as slides.

What to practise

The most mechanism- and identity-driven exam of the eight. On 335 complete items: mechanism 17.9% (highest of eight), enzyme or protein identity 11.0% (highest), diagnosis 11.0%, regulation 6.9%, treatment 6.9%.

Practise the one pattern the inborn errors are built on: enzyme missing → metabolite accumulating → clinical presentation → which option names the enzyme. That single motion is the enzyme-identity 11.0% and the diagnosis 11.0% at once, and it is what the inborn-errors table is for.

Lecture 41 routes to the figure-heavy set — 39.1% vignettes, 93.5% five options, 36.5% carrying an image. Lectures 37 and 38 route to the long-stem, regulation-focused set. How solid is this

Stated so the plan can be trusted where it is strong. Every quantity above comes from a corpus of 16,283 question items drawn from 2018 to 2026, of which 3,937 parse as complete lettered multiple-choice questions with their options intact, plus 11,430 card notes and 2,319 catalogued files. Each item was routed by topic onto the 2026 lecture list.

Strong

  • The time budget and the inverse distribution. n = 3,937 complete items, and the ordering holds at both ends across two independently constructed populations.
  • The format divergence between faculty and teaching-assistant questions. Six lecture sets, n = 155 to 1,744 each, same direction every time.
  • The map region assignment. Every region corroborated by where its vocabulary lands, several above 90% concentration in a single module.
  • The source-type yield rates (n = 384 to 7,020 per type) and the card-deck off-syllabus rates (11,430 notes).
  • The faculty topic and disease lists are not inference at all. They are the documents the people who set the paper wrote, transcribed verbatim.

Thin, and hedged where it appears

  • The haemoglobin question profile rests on n = 19 complete items — the weakest evidence in the analysis attached to the most reliably examined topic in the archive. Prepare for lab-value interpretation because the topic list asks for it; treat the 60-word median and the 26% treatment rate as hints.
  • The DNA-block profile rests on n = 27. Its four-option finding is indicative only.
  • CP 1's fine-grained topic weights are the least well resolved of the eight modules.
  • The low-footprint primers are judged on an absence of questions in a collection that is 87% teaching-assistant and interactive material. Teaching assistants may simply not review structural primer content. That is why the instruction is read once rather than ignore.

Not knowable from this material

  • What any 2026 exam will contain. Only 19 items in the whole corpus carry a 2026 date, none of them complete, covering three modules. Every forward-looking statement here is 2018–2025 material inferred onto a syllabus that was reorganised for 2026. The routing is explicit and documented; the transfer is an assumption.
  • How any individual teacher writes questions, with one exception. The profiles above describe what questions on that content look like — items routed by topic to a lecture, and from there to that lecture's owner. Only one lecture set has enough of its author's own questions to measure directly, and that is said in place where it appears. No habit in this plan should be attributed to a named person.
  • Anything about the graded Canvas self-assessment quizzes. Their question banks are not readable from a student account.

Two things about the percentages

  • They are flag rates, not a partition. "Renal, mechanism 17.9%" means 17.9% of its complete stems contain mechanism-seeking language. The figures do not sum to 100 and the unflagged remainder is large.
  • Image presence was recorded; no figure was ever transcribed. So the one-in-four figure load on Foundations 2 can be anticipated but not rehearsed from question text alone. Open the interactive decks as slides.
