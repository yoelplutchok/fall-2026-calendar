  • Working through the archive in the order it presents itself over-studies GI roughly fourfold and under-studies Foundations 2 to about a third of what it deserves. GI carries 4.8% of the grade and 18.2% of the usable material. Foundations 2 carries 26.3% of the grade and 9.7% of the material. Volume index is the share of the archive divided by the share of the grade; anything above 1.0 will absorb more time than it repays.
  • Time should track points, not availability. For every hour spent on GI, five and a half belong to Foundations 2. The per-week guidance below is written to that split rather than to what happens to be lying around.
  • A refinement worth carrying. The table counts every clean question routed to a 2026 module, including rows that originate in Spring Medical Genetics and Spring Endocrine/Reproductive files. Restricted to Fall-course material only — 2,308 clean rows — the two ends hold but two middle rows move: MSK 1 falls to 1.16, because its apparent over-supply was mostly Spring genetics contamination, and CP 1 rises to 1.79, making it the most over-supplied module after GI. The correlation weakens to −0.571 and stays negative. GI highest and Foundations 2 lowest is true on both counts.
  • Two probable causes, neither confirmed: GI absorbed heme, iron, vitamins and insulin from prior-year exams that were worth far more points then; and Foundations 2 sits two weeks into the semester, before the teaching assistants who write most of the archive have accumulated anything.

The method, and why this course needs its own

The recurring theme in advice from previous years is that a single study system will not carry all of first year, and that Biochemistry sits at the opposite end from Anatomy and Physiology. That still holds. What follows keeps the advice that the archive supports, corrects the three claims it contradicts, and marks the ones no measurement can settle either way.

  • This is a bottom-up course. Anatomy and Physiology reward building the framework first and filling in detail afterwards. Biochemistry does the reverse: the details come first, and the structure only appears once enough of them are in place. The first pass through a lecture will not feel like understanding, and that is the expected experience rather than a warning sign. It typically takes three or four passes before a block coheres. No corpus evidence either way; carried forward on the consistency of the reports.
  • Corrected. "Every number, diagram and figure on a slide is examinable" is not true, and acting on it wastes the scarcest resource you have. On 2,634 usable archived questions, 3.3% turn on a specific number, 1.4% ask a calculation, 0.6% require reading a figure. On faculty-written questions the rates roughly double — 5.8%, 2.2%, 3.8% — so past teaching-assistant reviews do understate the demand, but the numbers stay small. Meanwhile language about central concepts outnumbers minor-detail language by more than twenty to one. The right instruction is: read every slide completely, memorise the numbers the posted topic lists name, and expect one or two arithmetic items per exam. Volume is still the difficulty — but it is volume of mechanism, not volume of trivia.
  • Start each block from the faculty's own topic and disease list, not from the slides. This is the single largest change to the advice on this page. Sixteen of these documents exist across the eight modules, they are the shortest things in the archive, they are written as testable objectives, and several are effectively question stems already. They also define the option pool: when a question's five options are disease names — which happens in 10.7% of questions — the names come from that list. 2026 versions are posted for Foundations 1 and 2; the other six are 2025 and carry a boundary caveat.
  • Questions are the primary study activity, not the final check. Confirmed, with the ordering revised. Work from broadest to most faithful: the faculty topic list (coverage), then teaching-assistant reviews (breadth — 49 of 50 topics, 1.9% off-syllabus), then the faculty review deck (the highest-yielding source in the archive at 57.8% usable), then the interactive-session decks opened as PowerPoint (the only source matching the exam's format). The old ordering put the Canvas practice quizzes fourth. They are free and the graded ones carry 2% of the grade, so attempt them — but 403 of them were never extractable and the 14 that reached the corpus were all unusable, so there is no evidence they prepare you for anything.
  • Corrected, and it reorders the whole semester. Do not study in proportion to how much material exists. The archive is distributed inversely to the marks, at a correlation of −0.881. GI is 4.8% of the grade and 18.2% of the practice material; Foundations 2 is 26.3% of the grade and 9.7% of the material. Following the archive means spending roughly four times too long on GI and about a third of the right amount on the largest exam of the term. Track points, not availability.
  • Learn the question form, not only the content — but learn it from the right source. Interactive-session questions do reappear in the same shape, and 28.4% of them recur across two or more years. Teaching-assistant questions do not: on the same lectures they run about 60% as long, carry half the numeric load, contain almost no figures, and default to four options where the faculty use five. Most of what is available to practise on is the second kind. Use it for coverage and never for calibration.
  • Speaking the material out loud is repeatedly named as the highest-return technique for this course — explaining a pathway to another person, or to nobody in particular. It surfaces the steps that were being skimmed. No corpus evidence either way. Retained because it is reported consistently and costs nothing to test on yourself.
  • Stay one to two weeks ahead where the calendar allows. The empty weeks — Aug 10, Sep 28, Nov 16 — are the ones that make this possible, and they sit immediately before exams for a reason. Week 14, after the Nov 2 exam, is the fourth and it is the one that decides how the Renal exam goes.
  • Filter by course of origin before you drill anything. The archive contains Spring Medical Genetics and Spring Endocrine and Reproductive material that looks like the best content in the collection — one Spring review ranks second in the entire archive for breadth — and belongs to different courses. The Fall 2025 folder names also do not match 2026's modules, because the course was resequenced.

Working the metabolic map

The map is supplied in every exam, which is why the old advice put it in the first tier. The advice was right about the map and wrong about the timing.

  • It is an exam aid, so the skill being tested is navigation under time pressure. Knowing roughly where a pathway lives is worth more than having memorised its contents, because the contents are printed in front of you.
  • Corrected. It is a late-semester instrument, not a first-week one. One of its 23 regions is examined before Aug 17 and four more before Aug 31. Then nothing for six weeks — MSK 1 and MSK 2 need none of the 23 between them, which is 31 points with no map content. Three regions before Oct 19, one before Nov 2, two before Nov 23, and then eleven for the Renal exam alone. Learning the map "early" means learning five regions in August and leaving the largest commitment for December, which is exactly where the calendar is tightest. The dated region-by-region schedule is on the "What the exams ask" tab.
  • Corrected. Do not study from the annotated copy until October. The old advice was to work from the annotated version rather than the blank one. Its 127 text annotations are Renal and GI disease labels: there are zero annotations in the glycolysis, glycogen, pentose-phosphate, oxidative-phosphorylation, cholesterol, urea-cycle and ketone-body regions. For the two Foundations exams it adds nothing. Use the blank FullSubwayMap222.pdf until November, then switch — because on the Renal regions the annotations are precisely the disease names the exam asks for.
  • Before each exam, find the relevant region and work through it deliberately, so that no time is spent searching during the exam itself. The goal is still to look at the blank map and see the labels.
  • The parts that repeatedly catch people out, and none of them are content: the legend in the bottom-left corner, the vitamin names and abbreviations and their precursors, which side of the mitochondrial membrane each reaction sits on, the direction of the arrows, and the cofactors.
  • One region, steroid hormone synthesis, has no 2026 lecture at all. Skip it.

Exam technique

Carried over from previous years, with the pieces the archive can check now marked as checked.

  • Stems run long, and how long depends on the exam. Confirmed and quantified: faculty-written questions run a median of 43 words with 43.5% at 50 words or more. On Foundations 2 the median is 49 and half the paper runs 50-plus. Read the question, form an answer before looking at the options, then work the options against it.
  • Expect five options from the faculty even though your practice material gave you four. 70–94% of faculty questions on a given lecture set are five-option; teaching-assistant decks run 32–58%. A fifth option changes how elimination feels under time pressure.
  • Drop the "all of the above" and "none of the above" heuristics entirely. They appear in 1.3% and 0.3% of questions in this course, and the faculty use "none of the above" zero times in 685 questions. Combined "A and B" options appear in 0.2%. Negative stems — EXCEPT, NOT, LEAST — are 2.1%. Whatever rules of thumb you carry about those constructions are dead weight here.
  • When the answer is not known outright, eliminate — and how well that works varies by author. The old claim that most questions come down to two defensible options is true on average: option similarity across faculty questions averages 0.127. But it is not true everywhere. The one directly measured faculty profile in the course builds the least similar options of any set — median 0.015, with only 9.6% of items above 0.25. Five plainly different answers, one right. On his material, elimination is fast; on other material, expect the near-miss pair.
  • Know which pool the options are drawn from before you read them. Across all questions: 10.9% have enzyme names as options, 10.7% disease names, 8.3% paired increase-and-decrease, 5.1% purely numeric, 3.0% vitamins or cofactors. The disease-name pools are published — the Foundations 1 list is six lines long.
  • Flag and move on rather than stalling. Rank the flagged questions by difficulty and return to the easiest of them first.
  • Write down whatever is volatile as soon as the exam opens — a pathway, an enzyme list, a set of values.
  • Questions are written by different faculty, so one stem sometimes contains the information needed for another.
  • Keep a running file of missed questions and facts that will not stick, and read only that in the last hour before the exam.
  • Have the NBOME reference values to hand while practising anything acid-base or haematology. They are supplied in the exam, so practising without them trains the wrong task.
