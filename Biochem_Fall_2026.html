What to use, in order

There is more material available than any one person can work through. This is a ranking, not a list — the tiers are meant to be dropped from the bottom when time runs short.

Tier 1 — the course is written from these

Everything examined comes from here. Nothing below this tier substitutes for it.

  1. The Touro lecture slides and videos. Every testable fact lives in them, including the numbers and the diagrams. The consistent advice across every year is to read every bullet rather than skim for themes, and to expect three or four passes before it coheres.
  2. The Metabolic Map, learned early. It is provided in every exam, which makes fluency with it worth more than memorising it. Learn the legend, the vitamin abbreviations, which compartment each reaction occupies, and the direction of the arrows. Study from an annotated copy rather than a blank one; the annotated version in the archive is the same official map with the labelling filled in.In the repo: SGA - upperclassmen resources/Kevin MAP.pdf, alongside the blank FullSubwayMap222.pdf from Canvas.
  3. Interactive-session questions, current and archived. Written by the faculty who write the exams, which makes them the closest available proxy. The Fall 2025 archive files them per exam and even splits them by faculty member, so the individual question styles are visible.
  4. The fourteen primers. Short, and the recurring report from previous years is that each reliably produces at least one exam question. The Canvas module names state which primers belong to which exam, so there is no guesswork.
  5. The clinical vignettes. They generate questions that appear nowhere else and are among the easiest points available. Read them close to the exam so the details are fresh. Each is named in the module title of the exam it belongs to.
  6. The Canvas practice and self-assessment quizzes. Roughly thirty ungraded practice quizzes carry unlimited attempts and cost nothing. The graded ones are worth 2% of the course as well.

Tier 2 — high value, use as much as the week allows

Second-order material that consolidates rather than introduces.

  1. TA reviews. Twenty teaching assistants are assigned to the course this year, and the archive holds their review decks per exam. Reported as the best question source after the faculty's own. Made by students, so occasional errors are expected rather than alarming.
  2. Faculty review sessions and decks. Worth attending and worth reading afterwards. One specific warning carried forward from previous years: the course director's review deck differs in small ways from her lecture slides, and the differences are where the attention should go.
  3. Lippincott Illustrated Reviews: Biochemistry, 9th edition. The required text, and the syllabus maps chapters to individual lectures. Useful for a topic that has not landed after two passes of the slides, rather than as a primary read.
  4. The supplemental sessions. Optional, roughly weekly, and the one before each exam functions as a review. Questions asked there tend to be the ones the class collectively got wrong.

Tier 3 — targeted use only

Reach for these to unstick one topic, not to cover the course.

  1. Ninja Nerd videos for a mechanism that refuses to click after the slides and the textbook.
  2. First Aid for board-style framing of a topic already learned.
  3. Student-made reviews and Anki decks from the archive, which exist for each exam. Useful late, as a check on coverage.

Tier 4 — deliberately skipped

Not because they are bad, but because they are not aimed at this exam.

  1. External question banks and pre-made Anki decks. The consistent report from previous years is that they underperform for this course specifically, because the questions are written against a general biochemistry syllabus rather than against these slides. The same students recommend them for other courses.
  2. The optional textbooks — Panini, Marks, Molecular Biology of the Cell, Robbins. Listed in the syllabus as optional and reasonably treated that way.
  3. Building an original Anki deck for this course. Previous years describe the volume as too large to card usefully. The time is better spent on questions.

The method, and why this course needs its own

The recurring theme in advice from previous years is that a single study system will not carry all of first year, and that Biochemistry sits at the opposite end from Anatomy and Physiology.

  • This is a bottom-up course. Anatomy and Physiology reward building the framework first and filling in detail afterwards. Biochemistry does the reverse: the details come first, and the structure only appears once enough of them are in place. The practical consequence is that the first pass through a lecture will not feel like understanding, and that is the expected experience rather than a warning sign. It typically takes three or four passes before a block coheres.
  • Volume is the difficulty, not conceptual depth. Every number, diagram and figure on a slide is examinable. Colour-highlighted text matters but is not the boundary of what is tested.
  • Questions are the primary study activity, not the final check. The ordering that keeps being recommended runs from easiest and most general to hardest and most specific: textbook or review-book questions, then TA weekly reviews, then TA exam reviews, then the Canvas practice quizzes, then the faculty's own interactive-session questions. The last of those is the most predictive, because the people who wrote them write the exam.
  • Learn the question form, not only the content. Interactive-session questions tend to reappear in the same shape on the exam.
  • Speaking the material out loud is repeatedly named as the highest-return technique for this course — explaining a pathway to another person, or to nobody in particular. It surfaces the steps that were being skimmed.
  • Stay one to two weeks ahead where the calendar allows. The empty weeks — Aug 10, Sep 28, Nov 16 — are the ones that make this possible, and they sit immediately before exams for a reason.

Working the metabolic map

  • It is an exam aid, so the skill being tested is navigation under time pressure. Knowing roughly where a pathway lives is worth more than having memorised its contents, because the contents are printed in front of you.
  • Before each exam, find the relevant region of the map and work through it deliberately, so that no time is spent searching during the exam itself.
  • The parts that repeatedly catch people out: the legend in the bottom-left corner, the vitamin names and abbreviations and their precursors, which side of the mitochondrial membrane each reaction sits on, the direction of the arrows, and the cofactors.
  • Study from the annotated copy. The blank version is what appears in the exam, so the goal is to be able to look at the blank one and see the annotations.

Exam technique carried over from previous years

  • Stems run long. Read the question, form an answer before looking at the options, then work the options against it.
  • When the answer is not known outright, eliminate. Most questions have two defensible options; the work is finding which is worse.
  • Flag and move on rather than stalling. Rank the flagged questions by difficulty and return to the easiest of them first.
  • Write down whatever is volatile as soon as the exam opens — a pathway, an enzyme list, a set of values.
  • Questions are written by different faculty, so one stem sometimes contains the information needed for another.
  • Keep a running file of missed questions and facts that will not stick, and read only that in the last hour before the exam.
